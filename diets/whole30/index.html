Whole30

A structured 30-day elimination-and-reintroduction reset, not a long-term diet.

← Back to Diets

What Is Whole30?

Whole30 is a strict, time-limited elimination program: for 30 days it cuts out added sugar, alcohol, grains, legumes, dairy, and certain additives to see how removing them affects how you feel. It's not meant to be followed indefinitely — after the 30 days, those food groups are reintroduced one at a time in a structured process designed to reveal how each one individually affects your energy, sleep, digestion, and mood. In that sense it functions less like a typical diet and more like a short-term self-experiment or reset.

How It Works

Whole30 is a structured 30-day elimination protocol, not an ongoing diet. For 30 days it removes added sugar (real and artificial, including stevia, sucralose, and aspartame), alcohol, grains, legumes (including peanuts and soy), and dairy (ghee is an exception). It also has a rule nicknamed "SWYPO" ("Sex With Your Pants On" — the program's own deliberately memorable name), which flags compliant-ingredient junk-food mimics like paleo pancakes or almond-flour brownies: they're technically made of allowed ingredients, but the program considers them against the spirit of the reset because they recreate the psychological pull of treats it's trying to help you unlearn.

After the 30 days, foods are reintroduced one group at a time over a minimum 10-day period, returning to elimination for 2–3 days between each to isolate how that food affects energy, sleep, digestion, and mood.

Rules have changed over time — worth knowing if you've seen older summaries: Whole30 used to also exclude carrageenan, MSG, and added sulfites as food additives. It reversed all three of these exclusions (MSG in December 2021, carrageenan and sulfites in August 2024), citing insufficient evidence to justify banning them; the MSG reversal also specifically acknowledged the racially biased origins of the stigma against it. If a food list you've seen elsewhere still excludes these three, it's out of date.

The Evidence

Unlike every other entry on this page, Whole30 doesn't have independent, peer-reviewed clinical trials behind it. It's a company-created program, and after specifically checking for independent research on it, none exists: the only study of the Whole30 program by name is an unpublished, non-peer-reviewed pilot cohort of roughly 45 participants run by the company itself, plus self-reported alumni surveys. This isn't a gap we're softening — it's the single most important thing to know about this program before starting it.

Whole30's own claimed evidence: The Science Behind Whole30's Success — self-reported, company-affiliated data, not independent clinical trials.

Independent assessment: Cleveland Clinic's dietitian-reviewed overview frames Whole30 as "an experiment," not an evidence-backed treatment, and notes the near-total absence of peer-reviewed research on it specifically.

What is studied, separately from Whole30 itself, is the general elimination-then-reintroduction method it's built on — used clinically for identifying food allergies and sensitivities. That's a real, established diagnostic approach in its own right, but it's evidence for the general method, not proof that Whole30's specific 30-day version produces the health benefits it claims.

Food Examples

Compliant: chicken, beef, pork, fish, shellfish, eggs; all vegetables (potatoes, leafy greens, peppers, squash); fruit; compliant fats (olive oil, coconut oil, avocado, ghee); some nuts and seeds (almonds, walnuts, cashews — not peanuts, which are a legume).

Non-compliant: bread, pasta, rice, oats; beans, lentils, peanuts, soy and tofu; milk, cheese, yogurt; sugar, honey, maple syrup, artificial sweeteners; wine, beer, spirits; and SWYPO items like paleo pancakes or cauliflower pizza crust used as a treat substitute.

A Sample Day

Breakfast: scrambled eggs, sautéed spinach, avocado.

Lunch: grilled chicken over mixed greens with an olive oil-and-lemon dressing, roasted sweet potato.

Dinner: baked salmon, steamed broccoli, cauliflower rice.

Snack: apple with almond butter.

Who It Might Suit

May suit people who want a short, structured reset with a built-in process (reintroduction) for identifying personal food sensitivities. It's likely not well-suited to anyone with a history of disordered eating — Cleveland Clinic recommends provider clearance before starting for that reason — to vegetarians or vegans, since legumes and dairy are both excluded and leave few plant protein sources left standing, or to anyone looking for sustainable long-term structure: it's explicitly designed as a 30-day experiment, not a lifestyle, and continuing it indefinitely risks nutrient gaps from cutting legumes, dairy, and soy without a plan to replace what they provide.
