Popular Diets & Their Evidence

Popular, well-studied eating patterns

← Back
Diets, Studies & Food Resources

These summaries are for educational purposes only, not medical advice — talk to a doctor or registered dietitian before starting a new diet, especially if you have any health conditions.

Mediterranean Diet

▼
Emphasizes vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and olive oil as the primary fat, with fish and poultry over red meat, and wine in moderation. One of the most rigorously studied diets, linked to reduced cardiovascular disease, stroke, and all-cause mortality.

Study: PREDIMED Trial (NEJM, 2018) — a 7,447-person Spanish trial showing roughly 30% fewer major cardiovascular events.

Food & recipes: Oldways — Meal Plan, Food Lists & Recipes · Harvard Nutrition Source — Diet Review

DASH Diet

▼
Developed by NIH researchers, DASH emphasizes fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and low-fat dairy while limiting sodium, saturated fat, and sweets to lower blood pressure. Consistently ranked among the top diets for cardiovascular health.

Study: Original DASH Trial (NEJM, 1997) — a 459-person trial showing the combination diet significantly lowered blood pressure.

Food & recipes: NHLBI — DASH Eating Plan · NHLBI — A Week With the DASH Eating Plan

Intermittent Fasting

▼
Restricts the timing of food intake (e.g. a daily 16:8 window, 5:2, alternate-day) rather than what's eaten. Associated with weight loss comparable to calorie restriction, plus improvements in insulin sensitivity, blood pressure, and cholesterol — though long-term evidence is still limited.

Study: Effects of Intermittent Fasting on Health, Aging, and Disease (NEJM, 2019)

Food & recipes: Harvard Nutrition Source — Diet Review · Cleveland Clinic — Types & Schedules

Ketogenic (Keto) Diet

▼
A very-low-carbohydrate, high-fat diet (roughly 70–80% fat) that shifts metabolism toward ketone production. Originally developed for epilepsy, now popular for weight loss; shows short-term benefits similar to other calorie-restricted diets but no clear long-term advantage.

Study: Ketogenic Diet in Obese Patients — Systematic Review & Meta-Analysis (Nutrients, 2021)

Food & recipes: Harvard Nutrition Source — Diet Review · Mayo Clinic Diet — Healthy Keto Meal Plan

Plant-Based / Vegan Diet

▼
Centers on fruits, vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts, and seeds, excluding or minimizing animal products. Associated with lower BMI, lower rates of type 2 diabetes and heart disease, and lower all-cause mortality in large cohort studies.

Study: Vegetarian Dietary Patterns and Mortality (JAMA Internal Medicine, 2013) — a roughly 73,000-person Adventist Health Study cohort.

Food & recipes: Harvard Health — Becoming a Vegetarian · WebMD — Plant-Based Diet Tips for Beginners

Paleo Diet

▼
Modeled on presumed hunter-gatherer eating: lean meats, fish, fruits, vegetables, nuts, and seeds, excluding grains, legumes, dairy, and processed foods. Small trials show short-term improvements in glucose tolerance, waist circumference, and cardiovascular risk factors.

Study: Paleolithic vs. Mediterranean-like Diet on Glucose Tolerance (Diabetologia, 2007)

Food & recipes: Cleveland Clinic — Paleo Diet 101 · WebMD — Paleo Diet Guide

MIND Diet

▼
Short for Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay, created by Rush University researchers — it hybridizes Mediterranean and DASH with extra emphasis on berries and leafy greens for brain health. Associated with slower cognitive decline and reduced Alzheimer's disease risk.

Study: MIND Diet Associated with Reduced Incidence of Alzheimer's Disease (Alzheimer's & Dementia, 2015) — up to 53% lower risk with strict adherence.

Food & recipes: Rush University — MIND Diet Overview · Cleveland Clinic — The MIND Diet

Whole30

▼
A structured 30-day elimination protocol, not an ongoing diet — it removes added sugar, alcohol, grains, legumes, and dairy for 30 days, then walks you through reintroducing them one at a time to see how each affects you. Unlike the other entries on this page, Whole30 doesn't have independent peer-reviewed clinical trials behind it: it's a company-created program, and its own cited evidence is limited to a small unpublished pilot cohort and self-reported alumni surveys — worth knowing going in.

Evidence: Whole30 — The Science Behind Whole30's Success — self-reported, company-affiliated data, not independent clinical trials.

Food & recipes: Whole30 — Original Program Rules · Whole30 — The Official "Can I Have...?" Guide

Flexible Dieting / IIFYM

▼
Short for "If It Fits Your Macros" — no foods are off-limits; the only rule is hitting your daily protein, carb, and fat targets, which you can track directly with Nawtch's own macro tracking and calculators. The related academic concept is "flexible" vs. "rigid" dietary restraint, studied since the 1990s — flexible restraint (adaptable, no all-or-nothing rules) is associated with better long-term adherence and weight maintenance than rigid restraint, though IIFYM as a popular fitness term isn't identical to the studied construct, and most of that supporting evidence is correlational rather than causal.

Study: Conlin et al., Flexible vs. Rigid Dieting RCT (JISSN, 2021) — a 20-week trial finding flexible and rigid dieting produced similar fat loss during active dieting, with the flexible group retaining more lean mass afterward.

Food & recipes: Healthline — IIFYM Guide · Westenhoefer et al. — Flexible vs. Rigid Restraint (Int J Eat Disord, 1999)
